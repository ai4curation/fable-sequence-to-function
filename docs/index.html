General-purpose language models are neurosymbolic reasoners able to annotate proteins from sequence

Frontier general-purpose language models, given nothing but an amino-acid sequence, can place protein domains, spot a lost catalytic residue, and predict structure well above chance. They do it in one answer, combining pattern recognition over raw sequence with symbolic biological reasoning.

Marcin P. Joachimiak · Christopher J. Mungall — Environmental Genomics and Systems Biology Division, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory

01 — QUESTION

Two kinds of model, one task

Working out what genes do across the tree of life is one of biology's central problems. AI approaches to it fall into two camps. Protein language models learn from sequences and predict structure and function from patterns in them. General-purpose language models learn from text and reason about biology in words. The working assumption has been that each is poor at the other's job.

Recent reports showed that some frontier general-purpose models predict the next residue of obscure proteins far better than chance. We asked whether that sequence knowledge goes further: can these models actually annotate a protein from its sequence alone?

Sub-symbolic

Pattern in a string

Finding a domain, a motif or a helix in raw residues. People can't do this by reading; it's the job profile HMMs and protein language models were built for.

Symbolic

Knowledge and rules

“A kinase that has lost its catalytic-loop aspartate is unlikely to be active.” Curators and annotation rules work at this level, and generalist models are known to be good at it.

neurosymbolic: both in a single model, in a single answer

03 — RESULTS

What the models could do

A single catalytic substitution is noticed, and a matched decoy is ignored PSEUDO

The three frontier models called every wild-type kinase active and every kinase with its catalytic aspartate changed to asparagine inactive. That could just mean “any change looks broken”. So for the Claude models we made a decoy for each mutant: the same kind of substitution in the same protein, but at a position with no known function.

Two places where the models may be right and the database wrong

For two enzymes, all three frontier models put the catalytic residue at a different position from UniProt, and so called our “mutant” active. Both UniProt positions were transferred by similarity. We checked them with alignments to reviewed relatives and with structural models.

YrfG nucleotidase

P64636 · HAD superfamily
UniProt: Asp9Models: Asp16

All seven aligned relatives with an experimentally supported nucleophile map to Asp16. In structural models Asp16 sits 3.3 Å from the magnesium ion; Asp9 is more than 20 Å away.

Neither position has been tested by experiment. These cases show a sequence read can flag a misplaced site for a curator; two cases don't give a rate.

Domains are placed in raw sequence, and shuffles are left alone FEAT

The frontier models named the right Pfam family for most proteins and placed domain boundaries close to the reference. Given the same residues shuffled, they almost never assigned a domain. The earlier model, Opus 4.6, invented one for nearly half the shuffles.

Structure well above chance, short of dedicated predictors STRFN

For 12 proteins released after August 2026 with no close relative in the PDB, the frontier models beat the best single-state guess on secondary structure and far exceeded chance on long-range contacts. These values are well below AlphaFold-class predictors; the claim is only that the signal is there.

Function calls for the same 12 proteins were graded by hand: Fable 5.2 was right on 6, abstained on 5 and was wrong on 1; Opus 5.5 right on 4, abstained on 7, wrong on 1. Grading was unblinded and is being revisited with a finer rubric.

The next-residue signal isn't required NR

We replicated the next-residue advantage for Fable 5.2 and, at high effort, Opus 5.5. But Gemini 4 Neon showed no detectable advantage over shuffled sequence, and still did well on every annotation test. Annotation ability and this next-residue test come apart.

04 — OUTLOOK

A neurosymbolic annotator

Neurosymbolic AI combines sub-symbolic learning from raw data with symbolic knowledge and reasoning (Garcez & Lamb 2023; Kautz 2022). Protein annotation has always split the work this way: profile models find the domain, curators and rules decide what follows from it, and pipelines and people join the two.

The frontier models we tested did both in one answer. Their stated reasons need not reflect how they reached an answer, but the decoy control doesn't rely on them: the calls tracked the catalytic position whatever explanation came with them.

That suggests a different design for annotation review. One model reads the sequence, recalls what the literature and curated rules say about the family, weighs experimental data, and reports where they disagree, as it did for YrfG. Because its sequence reading is weaker than dedicated tools and has no provenance, the model proposes and tools check: an alignment to reviewed relatives, a predicted structure, the curated feature.

05 — CAVEATS

What this does not show

  • The tests are small. Cells hold 12 to 68 items, with at most one scored answer per model, setting and run.
  • Settings and routes weren't matched. Effort labels differ between providers; Claude went through a command-line tool, Gemini through provider APIs.
  • “Sequence only” rests partly on records. The original Claude route could admit extra context or connector tools, and its records can't fully rule that out. The mutants and wild types were asked again on a revised route that keeps user settings and connector tools out; Fable 5.2 and Opus 5.5 repeated 99 and 96 of 100 calls, and the decoys were asked only on that route.
  • Confident errors. Wrong answers are stated as confidently as right ones. A model's output is a flag for a curator, not sufficient evidence for an annotation by itself.
  • Mutant activity is assumed. We substituted curated catalytic residues; we didn't assay the mutants. The robust result is the difference between catalytic-site and decoy substitutions.
  • Gaps in the controls. No decoy control was run for Neon. The earlier model's decoys were asked at low effort only.
  • Recall is not ruled out everywhere. Wild-type Swiss-Prot proteins are well known. Shuffles, engineered mutants and decoys are the guards against recall.
  • Four models. Three frontier, one earlier. They don't stand for language models in general, and we know nothing about how they were trained.
  • “Novel” means no close earlier PDB match. Remote homologs were not excluded, and most sequences were already public.
